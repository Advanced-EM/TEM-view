Electron Explorer: field-trip and classroom guide

Electron Explorer is a cartoon electron microscope for grades K-12. Students turn on the beam, focus, zoom from a pollen grain down to single atoms, and take pictures for a sticker book, guided by Zip, a talking electron, in English, Spanish, Portuguese, French or German.

Learning goals

  • Scale. Students order objects from a hair (about 80 µm) to an atom (about 0.3 nm), and say how many times smaller each is.
  • Why electrons. Light waves are too long to show atoms. Electrons behave like much shorter waves, so an electron microscope shows far smaller things.
  • How a picture is made. Magnetic lenses bend electrons; thick parts of a sample stop more electrons and look dark. Real images are grey, and colors are added.
  • Scientific practice. Focus by trial and correction; use as little beam as possible, because a strong beam damages soft samples.

A 45-minute plan

TimeActivity
Before (10 min)Ask: "What is the smallest thing you have ever seen?" Show a hair under a magnifying glass. Students draw a guess for the size of a virus next to the hair (worksheet question 1).
Explore (20 min)In pairs, students play the missions: wake up the microscope, make it sharp, a close-up of pollen spikes, catch a virus, see real atoms, find the odd atom. One student drives, the other reads the mission and fills in the worksheet; swap halfway. Younger students can turn on Read aloud.
Discuss (10 min)Use the questions below. Compare stickers in cartoon colors and in "Real (grey)".
Wrap up (5 min)Each student names one thing they saw and how much smaller it is than a hair.

In a museum, plan for 3 to 5 minutes per visitor at the kiosk; the missions are short and can be done in any order after the first two.

Discussion questions

  • Why did the picture get blurry when you zoomed in, even though it was sharp before? (Focus matters more at high zoom.)
  • What happened when you turned the brightness all the way up on pollen? Why do scientists care? (Beam damage.)
  • The real pictures are grey. Is a picture with added colors still true? What should a scientist tell readers about it?
  • The phage injects DNA into a bacterium. Why might phages be useful to doctors? (Phage therapy against bacteria.)
  • Graphene is one atom thick. Can you think of something that is very thin but strong?

Good to know

  • The samples are drawn to scale from simple shapes, after real electron microscope images. They are not recorded images.
  • Nothing about students is collected online. On a museum kiosk, only anonymous daily totals are kept on that device.
  • For older students, the full simulator computes every image from the physics of a real transmission electron microscope.
