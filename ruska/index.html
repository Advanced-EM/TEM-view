Drive the first electron microscope

Berlin, November 1933. Ernst Ruska, 26, finishes a column of brass and magnetic coils that magnifies 12,000 times and shows detail finer than any light microscope. Pump it down, light the discharge, focus the coils, and take a plate before the beam burns your specimen.

    How it works

    Every behaviour of the simulated column follows the electron optics that Ruska described in 1934. The image is computed from these rules each frame.

    Lens

    A coil is a lens

    In 1926 Hans Busch showed that the magnetic field of a short coil bends electrons the way a glass lens bends light. Its focal length follows f ∝ U / (N I)²: turn up the current and the lens gets stronger. Ruska wrapped the coil in iron with a narrow gap, the pole piece, squeezing the field into a few millimetres. His objective focused 75 kV electrons at about 6 mm.

    Wave

    Waves 100,000 times shorter than light

    Ruska learned of de Broglie’s matter waves in 1931 and at first feared a new wavelength limit. The arithmetic reassured him: at 75 kV, λ = h / √(2meU) ≈ 4.3 pm, five orders of magnitude below visible light. In 1932 Knoll and Ruska predicted a limit of 2.2 Å. It was reached about 40 years later.

    Focus

    Change the voltage, lose the focus

    Because f depends on the voltage, any wander of the high voltage moves the focus, and its ripple smears every point into a disc (chromatic aberration). The gas discharge had to burn "extremely steadily". Watch the HV ripple bar grow when you open the discharge.

    Rotation

    The image turns as it grows

    Electrons spiral through a magnetic lens, so the image rotates by an angle that grows with the coil current. Turn the projector coil and the specimen spins as it magnifies. Ruska’s operators lived with it; modern microscopes compensate for it.

    Light budget

    Magnification costs light

    The image spreads the electrons over an area M² times larger. At 10,000× the specimen receives 108 times the current density of the screen. To see anything, the condenser must squeeze the beam onto a few micrometres. That heats the specimen, and an organic one chars.

    Contrast

    Contrast by scattering, not absorption

    Ruska noticed that even very thin specimens gave contrast. Not by absorbing electrons, but by scattering them out of the lens aperture. Thin specimens absorb little and stay cooler, which made biological electron microscopy possible.

    From a cathode-ray tube to 12,000×

    Ruska was an electrical-engineering student in Max Knoll’s group at the Technische Hochschule Berlin, which was building cathode-ray oscillographs to record very fast surges on high-voltage power lines. The microscope grew out of the problem of focusing their electron beams.

    The 1933 instrument ran on a research stipend of 100 Reichsmark a month. Looking back in his Nobel lecture, Ruska doubted that German science funding had ever bought an electron microscope more cheaply.
    1. 1924Louis de Broglie proposes that electrons are waves.
    2. 1926Hans Busch shows that a short coil acts on electrons like a convex lens on light.
    3. 1929In his student project Ruska records the first electron-optical images, of a 0.3 mm anode aperture, and wraps the coil in iron.
    4. 7 April 1931Two coils in series: a platinum grid imaged in two stages, 3.6 × 4.8 ≈ 17.4× at 50 kV. Today this tube is regarded as the first electron microscope.
    5. 1932Knoll and Ruska predict a resolution limit of 2.2 Å. Ruska and Bodo von Borries apply for a patent on the pole-piece lens.
    6. Nov 1933Ruska completes a two-stage microscope with condenser, objective and projector coils: 12,000×, and 50 nm resolved on a carbonized cotton fibre, beyond the light microscope.
    7. 1935–37Students working at the 1933 instrument record a house-fly wing (60 kV, 2,200×) and the diatom Amphipleura pellucida (53 kV, 3,500×, 130 nm).
    8. 1939Ruska and von Borries at Siemens deliver the first series-produced electron microscope, to Hoechst. Prototypes in 1938 reached 30,000×.
    9. 1986Ruska receives the Nobel Prize in Physics for his fundamental work in electron optics and the design of the first electron microscope.

    See it in Nuremberg

    A brass model of Ruska’s 1933 instrument, labelled Erstes Elektronen-Übermikroskop von E. Ruska, 1933, stands in the DemoSpace of the Electron Microscopy Museum Nuremberg. The museum keeps the microscopes of the following decades in working order, among them a Siemens ÜM 100 laboratory column derived from Ruska’s prototype.
